Our house dates from the very early 1900s. Technically this makes it Edwardian, but in terms of how it is built it shares many features with late Victorian homes. For simplicity, and because many of the challenges are the same, we refer to it as Victorian style throughout this guide.

Before we get started, we strongly recommend reading up on ventilation in older properties. Adding insulation without understanding how your home breathes can unintentionally create issues such as damp, condensation and mould. Insulation and ventilation must always be considered together, particularly in period buildings.

This guide focuses on: 
- Insulating a suspended timber floor
- What worked for us in our early 1900s property
- Practical lessons learned during the process

**A note before we begin**

This guide is not professional or technical advice. We are not surveyors or building professionals. Everything shared here is based on our own experience of insulating our home, alongside the research we carried out and the lessons we learnt along the way. Our aim is to help you better understand how your own property works so you can make more informed decisions. Knowledge is empowering, especially when dealing with older buildings.

## Benefits of installing underfloor insulation in your period home

Victorian and early Edwardian properties are notoriously difficult and expensive to heat. This is largely due to the way they were originally constructed. These homes were designed to manage moisture through airflow rather than airtightness. As a result, heat is easily lost through walls, floors and chimneys, and draughts and cold spots are common.

1. Reduced Heat Loss
Suspended floors can account for up to 10–15% of total heat loss in a poorly insulated older home.
Installing proper insulation reduces this heat loss significantly, especially in living spaces.
2. Lower Energy Bills
By preventing heat escaping through the floor, your heating system doesn’t need to work as hard.
Over time, this can make a noticeable difference on gas or electricity bills.
3. Increased Comfort
Floors feel warmer underfoot, especially in winter.
Eliminates cold spots and draughts caused by airflow through the underfloor void.
 

## Installation approach

Before starting, it’s important to decide how you will access the floor void. In suspended timber floors, there are two main options:

**Lifting the floorboards**
This approach gives full access to the joists and the underfloor void.
Ideal if you need to replace joists, clear debris, or do a thorough inspection, as we did.
Works best if your room is empty or furniture can be temporarily moved.
Keep the boards safe and labeled so you can reinstall them later. We labelled ours 1A, 1B, 2A etc (A being closest to our bay window wall). 

**Crawling under the floor**
If the void has enough headroom and is safe, you may choose to install insulation from below without removing floorboards.
Less disruptive, but can be trickier to fit insulation tightly around pipes, cables, or irregular joist spacing.

**Our experience:** We took all our floorboards up because we were replacing some joists and needed to clear the void completely. The room was unfurnished, so this was the simplest and safest option. Removing the boards allowed us to check every joist, fit insulation snugly, and ensure the floor was in good condition before putting it back together. 

## Materials
**Insulation**
When insulating suspended timber floors, a range of insulation materials are available. Choosing the right one depends on your home’s construction and the need to manage moisture.

1. Synthetic insulation boards
Made from rigid foam or polystyrene.
Easy to cut and install.
Highly insulating.
Consideration: These materials are impermeable, which can trap moisture and lead to condensation under suspended floors. This increases the risk of rot in timber joists if airflow is restricted.
2. Mineral wool (glass wool or rock wool)
Flexible and fitted between joists.
Some are treated for fire resistance.
Breathable to a degree, but can be less forgiving if compressed or wet.
3. Loose-fill insulation
Small particles, often cellulose or mineral-based, that are poured into voids.
Can fill irregular spaces well.
Requires careful installation to avoid gaps or settling.
4. Natural insulation (wool, hemp, cork)
Made from renewable, breathable materials.
Allows moisture to escape naturally, reducing condensation risk.
Flexible and easy to fit between joists in older homes.
Our experience: We used wool sheets treated for moths, which are particularly suited for suspended floors. Because the floor void is effectively “outside” due to air bricks, condensation can form on joists. Natural wool allows moisture to pass through rather than trapping it, helping prevent rot while improving thermal comfort.

### Why we used natural insulation for suspended floors:
- Maintains airflow and breathability, keeping joists dry.
- Reduces the risk of condensation forming under heated floors. If you think you are heating above the floor, and below will be ventilated from outside through the air bricks, condensation can appear in the insulation. Natural insulation does a better job of holding that moisutre and letting it go than its synthethic counterparts.
- Compatible with the historic construction of Victorian and Edwardian homes.
- Renewable and environmentally friendly.

## Materials
How to hold insulation in place
Suspending Insulation: Using Garden Netting and Membrane

For suspended timber floors, insulation needs to be held securely between the joists while still allowing airflow. We used garden netting to create “baskets” for our wool sheets, then placed a breathable membrane on top to help reduce draughts and protect the insulation.
The netting supports the wool while letting moisture pass through, which helps prevent condensation forming on the joists and reduces the risk of rot. By stapling the netting to the top of the joists with a hammer tacker and standard staples, we created stable baskets where the insulation could sit snugly and reach the top of the joist.

Once the insulation was in place, we rolled the a breathable membrane on top. This layer adds extra draught protection while remaining vapour permeable, allowing the floor to breathe. We also stapled the membrane with the hammer tacker to keep it secure over the insulation, taking care not to compress the wool. Overlaps were taped where necessary to maintain a continuous layer. We also ensured we had 150mm material when we reached walls as this will be held in place by the skirting boards, ensuring additional draught protection. 

### Key points

Ensure the wool insulation is treated against moths and check for signs of mice or rodents before installation.
Keep the underfloor void ventilated to allow airflow beneath the insulation.
Avoid compressing the insulation, as this can reduce performance and trap moisture.
Staple both netting and membrane securely to the joists using a hammer tacker.
Overlap the membrane by around 150mm for continuous coverage.
Using netting and a breathable membrane in this way made installation straightforward, kept the insulation stable, and allowed the floor to breathe while improving thermal comfort and protecting the joists.

**Tip from our experience:** Use smaller sections of membrane near radiators or key pipes if you need to access them you are able to do so by taking up minimal amounts of membrane or insulation.

## Putting the floor back
Once the insulation, netting and breathable membrane were securely in place, it was time to relay the floorboards.

Because we had labelled every board when lifting them (1A, 1B, 2A and so on), putting the floor back down was far more straightforward than expected. If you are removing original boards, careful labelling really does save hours of frustration later.

**Before relaying boards**
Before fixing anything back down, we:
- Double-checked that the insulation sat snugly between joists without being compressed
Ensured the membrane overlaps were secure and lying flat
- Confirmed no insulation was blocking air bricks or ventilation routes
- Checked pipework and electrics were correctly positioned and clipped
- This is your last easy opportunity to inspect everything — once the boards are down, access becomes much more disruptive.
  
**Refitting the boards**
- We started at one wall and worked our way across the room, keeping boards tight together to minimise gaps and draughts. In some areas, particularly where boards had slightly warped over time, we gently pulled them together before fixing them in place.
- Where boards had been damaged during removal, you can replace them with reclaimed timber of a similar age and size to maintain character and consistency.

**Fixing**
To stay true to the age of the house, we refitted the boards using traditional-style nails typical of the period. These were driven in and carefully countersunk below the surface, just as would have been done originally.

Using nails maintains the authentic feel of the floor and ensures the boards behave as intended in an older property. Countersinking them also allows for filling or sanding if required, while keeping the surface neat and in keeping with the character of the room.

**Tip from our experience:**
We deliberately cut some of our floorboards into smaller, discreet access sections around radiators and key pipe runs. This means if we ever need to access plumbing in the future, we only need to lift a small section rather than disturbing half the room.

## Summary & Conclusion
Insulating the suspended timber floor in our early 1900s Victorian-style living and dining room was one of the most impactful improvements we’ve made to our home.

Older properties like ours — technically Edwardian but built in a late Victorian style — were designed to breathe. They manage moisture through ventilation rather than airtight construction. Understanding that principle was essential before we started. Insulation without ventilation awareness can create damp problems rather than solve them.

### What worked well for us
- Lifting the floorboards completely to properly inspect the joists and clear the void
- Choosing natural wool insulation to maintain breathability
- Using garden netting to create stable support “baskets”
- Installing a vapour-permeable membrane to reduce draughts without trapping moisture
- Maintaining ventilation via air bricks
- Planning for future access by cutting strategic access panels
  
### Lessons learned
- Labelling boards carefully saves huge amounts of time
- Do not compress insulation — performance drops significantly
- Always think about future access to plumbing and services
- Ventilation must never be compromised
- Period homes require a balanced approach — warmth and breathability must work together
  
### The result
The rooms are warmer, more comfortable, and feel less draughty. Heating runs for shorter periods, and cold spots along external walls have reduced noticeably. Most importantly, we achieved this without compromising how the building manages moisture.

Insulating a suspended timber floor in a period property isn’t just about thermal performance — it’s about understanding how your house works as a system.

We are not professionals, but through research, care and patience, we were able to improve the comfort and efficiency of our early 1900s home while respecting its original construction.

If you are considering doing the same, take the time to understand ventilation, choose materials carefully, and plan thoroughly. With the right approach, it can be one of the most worthwhile upgrades you make to a Victorian or Edwardian property.
